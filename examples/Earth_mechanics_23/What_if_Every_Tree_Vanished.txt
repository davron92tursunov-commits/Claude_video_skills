TITLE
Every Tree Gone 🌳 What If All The Trees Vanished?

DESCRIPTION
What if every tree on Earth vanished? There are about three trillion trees today, and almost half of the trees that existed before human civilization are already gone.
You would not suffocate: the air holds about twenty-one percent oxygen, enough to last thousands of years.
The real problem is carbon. Forests absorb roughly a quarter of the carbon dioxide people release each year, and without them more of it would stay in the air.
Roots also hold soil in place, forests pump water into the air, and shade and evaporation cool cities by several degrees. Losing them would mean landslides, muddy rivers, drier rain belts and hotter cities.
About 1.6 billion people depend on forests for food, fuel or income.
Satellites show that Earth's leaf area has grown over the past twenty years, mostly in China and India.
The oxygen duration, the size of the cooling effect and the carbon, soil and rain effects are estimates.
Visuals are simplified and illustrative.
Map data: Natural Earth (public domain). 3D plants: Kenney Nature Kit (CC0). Tree count: Crowther et al., Nature, 2015.

FACT CHECK
- ~3.04 trillion trees; ~46 percent fewer than before civilization (Crowther et al. 2015, Nature). "Almost half already gone" OK.
- Oxygen is ~20.9 percent of the air; commonly cited estimates say it would last thousands of years without photosynthesis. ESTIMATE.
- Forests/land sink absorb roughly 25-30 percent of anthropogenic CO2 emissions (Global Carbon Project, Pan et al. 2011). "About a quarter" OK, rounded.
- Roots reduce erosion and landslides; forests recycle water (evapotranspiration); tree shade cools cities by several degrees (studies range about 1-5 C). ESTIMATE.
- 1.6 billion people depend on forests for their livelihoods (World Bank / UN). OK.
- Earth's leaf area grew ~5 percent from 2000 to 2017, driven largely by China and India (NASA / Chen et al. 2019, Nature Sustainability). "More green leaves than twenty years ago" OK.
